Technology landscape

Every serious route to printing in more than three axes, on one map.

A field becomes navigable when its options are named and compared honestly. This map is maintained as a public reference: routes, trade-offs, maturity — and the projects pursuing each route. It is deliberately incomplete, and it improves through contributions.

In short

There are six serious routes to printing in more than three axes: tilt–rotate (trunnion) tables and tilting heads — the two routes to true conformal, non-planar layers; 3+2 indexing, which re-orients between flat-layer sections; belt printers, which add one endless fixed-angle axis; robotic-arm extrusion for metre scale; and 5-axis print-and-mill hybrids. They trade usable volume, stiffness, accuracy, cost and maturity against each other, and no route wins on all five. This page compares them side by side and lists the projects pursuing each — a November 2026 snapshot that is openly incomplete.

Tilt–rotate table (trunnion) FDM

Route 1 · True conformal

The part rides a table that tilts and rotates beneath a conventional 3-axis gantry, so the surface being printed is always presented to the nozzle at the right angle. Strengths: stiff, compact, genuinely simultaneous 5-axis motion; the extruder stays upright, so filament feeding behaves normally.

Limits: the usable volume shrinks as the table tilts; tall parts swing through a large envelope; collision checking between part, table and gantry is mandatory.

Tilting / swivelling print head

Route 2 · True conformal

The rotary axes live in a wrist at the nozzle, which leans to stay normal to the surface while the part sits still. Strengths: works over a full-size flat bed; large parts don't move; natural fit for printing onto big stationary substrates.

Limits: the head itself becomes the collision problem — hotend, fans and cabling sweep a wide cone; extruders fed at steep angles behave badly; wrist stiffness limits speed and quality.

Research

3+2 indexed printing

Route 3 · Indexed

Re-orient the part between sections; print each section with ordinary flat layers. Strengths: by far the simplest control and slicing of any multi-axis route; reaches faces a 3-axis machine can't; reduces supports substantially.

Limits: layers are still flat within each section, so stair-stepping remains; seams and registration errors appear where orientations meet; not conformal printing, despite frequent marketing to the contrary.

Research · niche tools

Belt & continuous-axis printing

Route 4 · One extra axis

A conveyor-belt bed (or a continuously rotating fixture) prints at a fixed 45°-style angle, giving an effectively infinite axis for very long parts and natural support-free overhangs in one direction. Strengths: commercially available, affordable, excellent for long profiles and batch production of identical parts.

Limits: a single fixed orientation — no second rotary axis, no surface-following layers. An adjacent technology to 5-axis printing, not a subset of it.

Commercial niche

Robotic-arm extrusion

Route 5 · Large scale

An industrial robot arm (six or more degrees of freedom) carries an extruder, often pellet-fed, over metre-scale parts. Strengths: unmatched reach and orientation freedom; prints furniture-, boat- and building-scale geometry; non-planar toolpaths at architectural scale.

Limits: arm stiffness and path accuracy are far below gantry machines; cost, cell footprint and safety requirements exclude most labs; fine conformal detail is not its game.

Early commercial · large format

5-axis hybrid: print + CNC mill

Route 6 · Hybrid

One machine deposits material with multi-axis motion, then machines it to tolerance in the same setup. Strengths: the only route that pairs conformal geometry with machined surface finish and accuracy; powerful for tooling and end-use metal-adjacent workflows.

Limits: highest cost and complexity on this map; two process disciplines to master; mostly aimed at industrial users rather than research exploration.

Early commercial · industrial

Projects & players worth knowing

A snapshot of the field as of November 2026, across academia, open source and industry. Inclusion is informational, not endorsement — and the list is openly incomplete. Missing someone? Tell us.

Open5xImperial College London · open source

An open-source tilt–rotate hardware add-on that converts a conventional desktop printer into a 5-axis machine, with non-planar slicing built on Rhino/Grasshopper. The reference point for the DIY-and-research end of Route 1, and proof that the retrofit path is real — controller and slicer included.

5AxisMakerLondon · desktop hybrid

One of the earliest desktop machines to combine 5-axis CNC milling and 3D printing in a single unit — an early commercial attempt at Route 6, years before "hybrid" became a category.

Ai BuildLondon · robotic cells

Large-format robotic printing cells and the software to drive them — a well-known name on Route 5, focused on industrial and architectural-scale parts.

Branch TechnologyUSA · construction scale

Robotic "cellular fabrication": freeform lattice structures printed with arms and assembled into architectural walls — Route 5 pushed to building scale.

Blackbelt & Creality CR-30Belt printing · commercial

The machines that made Route 4 real products: conveyor-bed printers for infinite-length parts, widely used for batch profiles — and the most common source of "isn't that 5-axis?" confusion this page exists to clear up.

University research groupsWorldwide · slicing & motion

Much of the field's slicing theory — orientation fields, collision-free non-planar path generation, multi-axis process planning — comes from university groups publishing papers and prototypes rather than products. Our own slicer work with institute and university partners since 2022 sits in this tradition.

Questions this page answers

How many routes are there to multi-axis 3D printing?+

Six serious ones: tilt–rotate (trunnion) tables, tilting print heads, 3+2 indexing, belt and continuous-axis printing, robotic-arm extrusion, and 5-axis print-and-mill hybrids. They differ in orientation freedom, stiffness, usable volume, cost and maturity.

Which routes can I buy commercially today?+

Belt printers are established commercial products, and robotic-arm cells and hybrid machines are early commercial offerings aimed at industrial users. True conformal routes — trunnion tables and tilting heads — remain research projects and early, built-to-order machines, including our own prototype platform.

Is this landscape complete?+

No — and it says so on purpose. It is a snapshot of the field as of November 2026, maintained as a public reference and corrected through contributions. If a route or project is missing, that is a bug in this page, and the contribution path is how it gets fixed.
