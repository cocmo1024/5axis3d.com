Reading the landscape: six routes to multi-axis

In short

“Multi-axis printing” covers six distinct routes: trunnion-table FDM, tilting-head machines, 3+2 indexing, belt printers, robotic-arm extrusion and print-and-mill hybrids. Only the first two produce true conformal (non-planar) layers; 3+2 keeps layers flat within each section, and belt printers add a single fixed-angle axis rather than rotary re-orientation. The routes differ in usable volume, stiffness, collision behaviour, cost and maturity — which is why the honest comparison is by route, not by axis count.

"Multi-axis printing" covers machines with very different ambitions. Trunnion-table FDM (our route) targets true conformal layers at bench scale. Tilting-head designs keep a big flat bed but fight head collisions. 3+2 indexing is the pragmatic middle. Belt printers add one endless axis for long parts at a fixed angle — useful, but not conformal. Robotic arms trade accuracy for metre-scale freedom. Print-and-mill hybrids buy surface finish with a machining pass, at hybrid prices.

The full comparison — strengths, limits, maturity, and named projects for each route — is in the technology landscape below. If a route or project is missing, that is a bug in this page, and the contribution path is how it gets fixed.

Questions this page answers

Which multi-axis routes produce truly curved layers?+

Two of the six: trunnion-table FDM and tilting-head machines, because both re-orient continuously during deposition. 3+2 indexing keeps layers flat within each section, and belt printers print at one fixed angle, so neither is conformal printing.

Is a belt printer a 5-axis printer?+

No. A belt printer adds one continuous axis at a fixed angle — excellent for very long parts — but it has no second rotary axis and cannot follow a surface. It is an adjacent technology to 5-axis 3D printing, not a subset of it.

Which route should a research lab choose?+

For bench-scale conformal research, the trunnion-table route is the most direct: stiff, compact, genuinely simultaneous, and the extruder stays upright. Robotic arms suit metre-scale work, and hybrids suit teams who also need machined finishes. The landscape page compares all six routes in detail.
